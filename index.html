The Big Picture

Language has always changed — but never this fast

Social media has created the fastest period of language change in human history. Words that didn't exist five years ago are now in the dictionary. Let's explore how and why.

New Words

TikTok as a Dictionary

Terms like slay, rizz, understood the assignment, no cap and main character energy have jumped from TikTok into everyday speech — and into the Oxford English Dictionary. In 2023, "rizz" was named Oxford's Word of the Year.

Grammar

Lowercase as Style

Online writing breaks traditional rules intentionally. Using all lowercase (i can't even), leaving out punctuation, or typing in fragments signals tone and identity. Linguists call this "expressive typography" — it's not a mistake, it's a choice.

A New Layer of Meaning

Emojis don't replace words — they add emotional tone that written text can't easily show. A "😂" after a statement changes its entire meaning. Some linguists argue emojis function like a paralanguage, similar to tone of voice or facial expressions.

Quick Explainer

Is social media ruining English?

This is one of the most debated questions in modern linguistics. Older generations often worry that abbreviations, emojis, and slang mean young people can't write "properly." But most linguists disagree.

Language change is not language decay. Every generation adds new words and expressions. Shakespeare invented over 1,700 words. Text speak ("LOL", "OMG") was once considered dangerous — today it's in the dictionary.

What social media actually does is accelerate and spread language change. A new expression can go from one community to global use in days, instead of decades. That's genuinely new — and genuinely fascinating.

The real risk isn't that English is being "ruined." The real risk is that English's global dominance is squeezing out other languages. That's the conversation linguists are most concerned about.

🔬 Linguist's Take

"The internet hasn't made us worse at language — it has actually made many people more aware of language as a flexible, creative tool. Young people online are highly sophisticated language users." — Gretchen McCulloch, linguist and author of Because Internet

⚠️ The Other Side

Critics argue that heavy use of informal online writing may affect formal writing skills, especially for academic contexts. Some teachers report students using "u" instead of "you" or missing punctuation in essays. The debate continues.

🇳🇴 Norway Context

In Norway, the dominance of English on social media (especially gaming, music, and TikTok) is causing linguists to worry about language pressure on Norwegian — particularly Nynorsk. Young Norwegians often code-switch between Norwegian and English mid-sentence.

Rizz, Slay, No Cap: How TikTok Became a Dictionary

In 2023, Oxford Dictionaries named "rizz" — meaning charm or charisma — as Word of the Year. It originated in streaming culture and exploded on TikTok. This article examines how the platform accelerates word creation and global adoption.

Social media platforms, and TikTok in particular, have become the fastest engines of vocabulary change the English language has ever seen. A word can be created by one user on a Monday and be understood globally by Friday.

How does this work? TikTok's algorithm connects users based on interests rather than geography. This means a teenager in Birmingham, a student in Lagos, and a surfer in Sydney can all be part of the same linguistic community — sharing, remixing, and spreading the same expressions.

Semiotics~6 min read

More Than Words: Emojis, Memes, and the New Language of the Internet

Are emojis a language? Can a meme communicate something a sentence cannot? This article explores the linguistics of visual communication in the digital age.

When the "Face with Tears of Joy" emoji 😂 was named Oxford's Word of the Year in 2015, many people laughed. But linguists took it seriously. Emojis had become so embedded in digital communication that ignoring them was ignoring how people actually write.

Emojis as paralanguage: In face-to-face conversation, we use tone of voice, facial expressions, and gestures to add meaning to our words. Written language traditionally lacks these. Emojis fill that gap. A message like "Yeah, that's fine" means very different things with 😊, 😐, or 🙂 at the end.

Interestingly, the same emoji can mean different things across generations. Research from 2021 found that younger users interpret 🙂 as passive-aggressive or even threatening — not friendly — because of how it's used ironically online. This is an example of semantic shift happening in real time.

Memes as cultural language: Memes work differently. They rely on shared cultural knowledge — you need to know the original context to understand the joke or message. Linguists see memes as a form of intertextuality: meaning built from references to other texts.

In 2024, the "Demure" and "Very mindful, very cutesy" trend on TikTok became a global phenomenon within days — demonstrating how internet language can now transcend culture at unprecedented speed.

Grammar~7 min read

Grammar Rules? What Rules? Intentional Deviation Online

Writing "i cant" instead of "I can't" isn't a mistake online — it's a stylistic choice. Explore how social media has transformed our relationship with grammar.

Traditional grammar teachers might despair at social media text. No capital letters. Missing apostrophes. Sentence fragments. Endless ellipses... But linguist Gretchen McCulloch, author of Because Internet (2019), argues that online writers are more conscious of grammar, not less — they're just using it differently.

Expressive typography: The choice to use lowercase signals a casual, intimate, sometimes ironic tone. "im fine" reads very differently from "I'm fine." The lowercase version implies something like the opposite of fine. This is a pragmatic use of typography — using the form of writing to convey meaning.

The period problem: In text messages and online chat, ending a short sentence with a period (full stop) has become a sign of formality, coldness, or even passive aggression. "See you later." feels very different from "see you later" or "see you later!" Research confirms this perception is widespread among younger users.

All caps for emotion: "I CANNOT BELIEVE THIS" uses capitalisation for emphasis, essentially simulating shouting. This is an example of written prosody — using spelling and punctuation to represent how speech sounds.

Write the same message in three styles: standard formal English, casual online style, and TikTok caption style. Notice how your word choices, punctuation, and even the ideas you express change. What does this tell you about the relationship between language and context?

Endangered Languages~9 min read

When English Takes Over: Language Loss in the Digital Age

The internet speaks mostly English. For speakers of smaller languages, this creates enormous pressure — and sometimes, language death.

Of the roughly 7,100 languages spoken in the world today, linguists estimate that half will be extinct by 2100. Social media and the internet are accelerating this process — but also, paradoxically, offering new tools for language revival.

The digital divide: Most social media platforms were designed for English. Algorithm-driven recommendation systems favour content in languages with large user bases. A post in Sámi (spoken by the indigenous Sámi people of Norway, Sweden, Finland, and Russia) competes for attention in the same system as a post in English — and almost always loses.

The Norwegian case: Norway has two official written standards — Bokmål and Nynorsk. Nynorsk, used by about 10-15% of the population, is under particular pressure. Young Norwegians increasingly consume English-language content on YouTube, TikTok, and Netflix, leading to what sociolinguists call language attrition — the gradual weakening of proficiency in one's native language.

English words enter Norwegian through social media constantly: chill, vibe, trigger, share, like, scroll — most Norwegians use these English words even when Norwegian equivalents exist.

Is this always bad? Linguists disagree. Some argue that language contact and borrowing is natural and has always happened. The question is one of power: when English words replace Norwegian ones because English dominates media and technology, that is not an equal exchange.

Fighting back: Some communities use social media to revitalise endangered languages. Welsh on TikTok has a thriving community. The Māori language (te reo Māori) has been championed by New Zealand content creators. Even Sámi languages have TikTok presence. These efforts show that digital tools can work both ways.

Sociolinguistics~10 min read

AAVE and the Internet: Credit, Culture, and the Spread of Black Language

Much of the language that defines online English culture has its roots in African American Vernacular English (AAVE). Understanding this is essential to understanding how social media changes language — and who benefits from those changes.

Words like slay, serving looks, tea (meaning gossip), drag, shade, yass, no cap, bussin', hits different, lowkey, and dozens of other expressions used globally online trace back to AAVE — the dialect spoken by many African Americans — or to Black and LGBTQ+ communities, particularly drag culture and ball culture (as seen in the TV show Pose or the documentary Paris is Burning).

How it spreads: These expressions gain mainstream visibility through social media, often first through Black creators on platforms like Vine, Twitter, and TikTok. They get picked up by non-Black users who may not know their origin — and sometimes those users gain more visibility and revenue from the same content than the original creators did.

The credit debate: In 2021, a viral moment on TikTok sparked major debate when non-Black creators were criticised for using AAVE expressions without acknowledging their origin. This led to broader conversations about linguistic appropriation — when the dominant culture adopts the language of a marginalised group, benefits from it, but doesn't credit or support that group.

The British dimension: Research published in 2022 by Dr. Devyani Sharma and colleagues at Queen Mary University of London found that AAVE features were spreading among young Black Londoners via social media — creating a transatlantic Black English influenced by both American and British Black vernacular traditions. This is a fascinating example of dialect levelling happening across an ocean.

🗣️ Discussion Question

Should social media users who adopt language from other cultures acknowledge the origin of those words and expressions? What are the arguments for and against? Is this different from borrowing vocabulary from other languages (like English borrowing from French)?

History & Context

How English became the world's shared language

English is today spoken by over 1.5 billion people as a first or second language. But this didn't happen by accident — it's the result of centuries of history, power, and technology.

1600s–1800s

The British Empire

At its peak, the British Empire covered about 24% of the world's land surface and ruled over 400 million people. Wherever the British went — India, Australia, Canada, South Africa, Nigeria, Hong Kong — they brought English with them. Education systems, courts, newspapers, and governments operated in English. Even after independence, many former colonies retained English as an official language because it was neutral between competing local languages or simply deeply embedded in institutions.

1900s

The American Century

After World War II, the United States emerged as the world's dominant economic and military power. American English spread through cinema, music (jazz, rock and roll, hip-hop), television, and later computing. The Bretton Woods financial system, the United Nations, NATO, and most international institutions used English. This "soft power" — cultural influence rather than military force — made English feel like the language of opportunity, modernity, and global access.

1990s

The Internet is Born in English

The World Wide Web was invented by British scientist Tim Berners-Lee, and almost all early internet content was in English. The major tech companies — Google, Amazon, Facebook (Meta), Apple, Microsoft — were American. The programming languages used to build websites and software (Python, JavaScript, HTML) use English keywords. This meant that to participate in the digital revolution, you needed English.

2000s–2010s

Social Media Globalises English

Facebook, Twitter, YouTube, and later Instagram were all designed for English first. As they spread globally, they brought English with them — not just as a language to use, but as a cultural context. English became the language of viral content, internet humour, and digital culture. Even in countries where English is rarely spoken, people used English hashtags, memes, and references to participate in global online conversations.

2020s

AI Reinforces English Dominance

Large AI language models like ChatGPT (released 2022) were trained overwhelmingly on English text — because most internet content is in English. This means AI tools work better, more reliably, and with more nuance in English than in other languages. As AI becomes embedded in work, education, and daily life, this creates a new structural advantage for English speakers — and a new pressure on other languages.

What is a lingua franca?

The term lingua franca (from Italian, meaning "Frankish language") originally referred to a pidgin language used for trade around the Mediterranean. Today it means any language used as a common means of communication between people who don't share a native language.

Throughout history, different languages have served as lingua francas in different regions: Latin in medieval Europe, Arabic across the Islamic world, Persian in South Asia and Central Asia, French in 18th–19th century diplomacy, and Swahili across East Africa.

English is unusual because it has become a global lingua franca — used across virtually every domain (science, business, aviation, diplomacy, popular culture) on every continent. Researchers call this English as a Lingua Franca (ELF).

Important: most English communication today happens between non-native speakers. An engineer in Tokyo talks to a supplier in Brazil in English. A scientist in Norway publishes research in English for readers in Kenya and South Korea. This means "standard" English is increasingly being redefined by its non-native speakers.

📊 Key Facts

~380 million people speak English as a first language.

~1.1 billion speak it as a second language — almost three times more.

This means English belongs more to its second-language speakers than to its native speakers. This is historically unprecedented.

🇳🇴 Norway's English

Norway consistently ranks as one of the world's top countries for English proficiency among non-native speakers (EF English Proficiency Index). This is partly due to subtitled (not dubbed) TV and film, high internet use, and a school system that prioritises English. Norwegian young people often report feeling more comfortable writing in English than in Norwegian online.

Varieties of World Englishes

English isn't one language — it's a family of related varieties. Linguist Braj Kachru described this using a model of three concentric circles.

CircleDescriptionExamplesApprox. Speakers
Inner Circle Countries where English is the primary native language UK, USA, Australia, Canada, New Zealand ~380 million
Outer Circle Countries where English is an official language due to colonial history India, Nigeria, South Africa, Singapore, Kenya ~300–500 million
Expanding Circle Countries where English is a foreign language, learned for international use Norway, China, Brazil, Germany, Japan ~600 million–1 billion
⚠️ The Power Question

English's status as a lingua franca is not neutral. It gives native English speakers an enormous advantage in global business, academia, and diplomacy. A French scientist must write in English to be read globally. A Norwegian student must take an English proficiency test to attend an American university. Critics argue this is a form of linguistic imperialism — the imposition of one language's dominance at the expense of others.

Reference

Key Words & Concepts

Master this vocabulary to discuss language and social media with precision. These terms are used by linguists, journalists, and researchers — and will elevate your English 2 essays and discussions.

Linguistics Core Terms
neologism
noun
A newly coined word or expression. Social media produces neologisms at an unprecedented rate.
"'Rizz' is a recent neologism that entered the Oxford English Dictionary in 2023."
semantic shift
noun phrase
When the meaning of a word changes over time. Can be broadening, narrowing, or complete reversal (pejoration/amelioration).
"The word 'sick' has undergone semantic shift — it now often means excellent rather than ill in youth slang."
code-switching
noun / verb
Switching between languages, dialects, or registers depending on the social context or audience.
"Norwegian teenagers often code-switch between Norwegian and English in the same sentence online."
sociolinguistics
noun
The study of how language varies and changes in relation to social factors like age, gender, class, ethnicity, and geography.
"Sociolinguistics helps explain why TikTok users and their grandparents use English so differently."
dialect
noun
A variety of a language spoken by a particular group, distinguished by vocabulary, grammar, and pronunciation.
"AAVE (African American Vernacular English) is a dialect of English with its own consistent grammatical rules."
register
noun
A variety of language used for a particular purpose or in a particular social setting (e.g. formal, informal, technical).
"Social media has created new registers — like 'TikTok caption language' — that are different from both formal and traditional informal English."
lingua franca
noun
A language systematically used to communicate between people who don't share a native language.
"English functions as a lingua franca in international science, business, and aviation."
language attrition
noun phrase
The gradual loss of proficiency in a language, often because another language is used more frequently.
"Linguists worry that heavy exposure to English content may cause language attrition in smaller language communities."
linguistic imperialism
noun phrase
The spread and dominance of one language (often English) over others, linked to economic and political power.
"Critics argue that the English-only default of most tech platforms is a form of linguistic imperialism."
lexical borrowing
noun phrase
When a language adopts a word from another language. Also called loanwords or borrowings.
"Norwegian has borrowed 'chill', 'vibe', and 'scroll' from English via social media — a form of lexical borrowing."
prescriptivism
noun
The view that language should follow strict, pre-defined rules. Opposite of descriptivism.
"Grammar police online often take a prescriptivist stance, insisting that there is one 'correct' way to write."
descriptivism
noun
The approach of studying language as it is actually used, without judging it as right or wrong. Most modern linguists are descriptivists.
"A descriptivist would say that using 'literally' to mean 'very' is not wrong — it simply reflects how the word is evolving."
Internet & Social Media Language
meme
noun
An image, video, phrase, or format that spreads virally online, often remixed to carry new meanings. Coined by biologist Richard Dawkins in 1976 to describe how ideas spread like genes.
"The 'This is fine' dog meme has become a shorthand for calm acceptance of a terrible situation."
hashtag
noun
A word or phrase preceded by # used to categorise content on social media. Has become a form of commentary and political action (#BlackLivesMatter, #MeToo).
"The #MeToo hashtag transformed a Twitter conversation into a global social movement."
emoji
noun
A small digital image or icon used to express an emotion, idea, or object in electronic communication. From Japanese: 絵 (e = picture) + 文字 (moji = character).
"Linguists study emojis as a form of paralanguage — adding emotional nuance to written text."
paralanguage
noun
Non-verbal elements of communication that convey meaning alongside words: tone of voice, facial expressions, gestures — and in writing, punctuation, capitalisation, and emojis.
"Ending a message with a period in casual chat functions as paralanguage signalling coldness or finality."
viral
adjective
Content that spreads very rapidly across the internet, reaching large audiences through sharing. The metaphor compares the spread of content to a biological virus.
"When a new slang term goes viral on TikTok, it can enter common usage globally within days."
netspeak
noun
The informal language used in online communication, characterised by abbreviations, emoticons, unconventional spelling, and new vocabulary. Term coined by linguist David Crystal.
"Crystal argued that netspeak is not a threat to English but a creative new dimension of it."
AAVE
abbreviation
African American Vernacular English. A dialect of English with its own grammar, vocabulary, and phonology, spoken by many African Americans. Source of much of the slang that dominates social media.
"Terms like 'no cap', 'slay', and 'on fleek' originate in AAVE before spreading to mainstream internet culture."
algorithmic amplification
noun phrase
The way social media algorithms promote certain content — and by extension, certain linguistic styles and communities — over others.
"English content benefits from algorithmic amplification because most training data and user bases are English-dominant."
linguistic appropriation
noun phrase
When speakers of a dominant language or culture adopt words, phrases, or styles from a marginalised group without acknowledgment or credit.
"The mainstream adoption of AAVE online is often cited as an example of linguistic appropriation."
digital native
noun
A person who has grown up with the internet and digital technology as a normal part of life. Originally coined by Marc Prensky in 2001 — though the concept has since been criticised as oversimplified.
"Digital natives often have very different intuitions about writing norms than those who learned to write before social media."
ephemeral content
noun phrase
Content designed to disappear after a short time (e.g. Instagram Stories, Snapchats). The temporary nature shapes the language used — more casual, unpolished, immediate.
"Ephemeral content encourages a different writing style than permanent posts — more like speech, less like edited text."
performative writing
noun phrase
Writing that is aware of its audience and performs identity, emotion, or attitude — common in social media captions and comments.
"'I'm literally crying 💀' is an example of performative writing — it signals amusement or relatability rather than literal distress."
Looking Ahead

The Future of English & Digital Language

What will happen to English — and to other languages — in the age of AI, social media, and global connectivity? Linguists have some predictions.

🤖

AI Changes Everything

AI writing assistants (ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude) are changing how people write. They tend to produce "standard" English, which may flatten linguistic diversity. But they also translate languages better than ever, potentially empowering non-English speakers.

📉

Language Death Accelerates

Linguists predict 50% of all languages could be gone by 2100. Social media and the global English internet accelerate this. When a language loses its economic and digital value, young speakers stop using it — even at home.

🌱

Language Revival Is Possible

Welsh, Māori, and Hebrew show that languages can be revived. TikTok content in endangered languages reaches young audiences globally. Communities that create compelling digital content in their languages can attract new speakers.

🔀

English Will Keep Changing

English itself is not static. Regional varieties — Indian English, Nigerian English, Singaporean English — are growing in confidence and influence. The idea of one "correct" English is weakening. "Global English" may look very different from British or American English.

🌏

Mandarin & Spanish Push Back

Chinese social media platforms (WeChat, Douyin/TikTok's Chinese version) are building parallel digital ecosystems in Mandarin. Spanish is the fastest-growing language on the US internet. English may face real competition for the first time in decades.

👾

New Digital Languages

Online communication continues to evolve: GIFs, reaction images, voice notes, short video — these are all changing what "language" even means. Future communication may rely less on text altogether. The question is: will AI and machines define the linguistic norms?

What happens when English takes over other languages?

The relationship between English and other languages is not a simple story of replacement. It's more complex — and more troubling in some ways.

Domain loss: The first sign of English taking over is when a language loses ground in important areas of life — first in business and technology, then in academia, then in entertainment. When young people can't discuss science, technology, or pop culture in their native language, it begins to feel inadequate for modern life.

Code-mixing: Many languages today are merging with English rather than disappearing. "Hinglish" (Hindi + English), "Spanglish" (Spanish + English), "Franglais" (French + English), and "Norwenglish" are all real phenomena. Is this enrichment or erosion? Linguists debate this intensely.

Linguistic inferiority complex: When English dominates digital life, speakers of other languages can start to feel their language is "lesser." Norwegian words feel less professional. Smaller languages feel embarrassing. This attitude — called language shame or linguistic insecurity — can accelerate language loss from the inside.

The academy vs. the internet: Formal language organisations (like Akademiet for de nordiske språk in Scandinavia or the Académie française in France) try to defend their languages by coining native equivalents for English loanwords. But social media users often ignore these efforts and just adopt the English word anyway.

⚡ Recent Example: France

In 2023, France passed a law banning the use of English gaming terms in official government communications. Words like "streamer", "esport", and "cloud computing" must now have French equivalents in official contexts. Critics called this futile; supporters called it necessary language defence. The debate illustrates the tension between organic language change and deliberate language policy.

🇳🇴 Norway's Language Policy

Norway has an official language policy that tries to maintain both Bokmål and Nynorsk as equal written standards. But critics argue the policy doesn't address the real challenge: that young Norwegians spend more time consuming English content online than Norwegian content. Language laws cannot compete with algorithms.

💬 Quote to Discuss

"When you lose a language, you lose a way of seeing the world. Every language encodes a unique set of concepts, histories, and ways of making meaning. Language death is not just a cultural loss — it's a cognitive one." — David Crystal, linguist

Strong academic content, connects language to social justice and policy. Highly relevant for the English-and-other-languages discussion.

Further Reading

Books & Articles

Book

Because Internet — Gretchen McCulloch (2019)

The definitive book on internet language, by a linguist who argues that online writing is creative, complex, and worthy of serious study. Funny, readable, and incredibly relevant. Highly recommended for English 2 students.

Book

Language and the Internet — David Crystal (2001)

An early but still highly relevant look at how internet communication differs from speech and writing. Crystal argues that "netspeak" is a new dimension of English, not a corruption of it.

Article

"How TikTok Is Changing the English Language" — The Atlantic (2022)

Accessible journalism exploring how TikTok's For You Page algorithm creates global linguistic communities, and why English-based slang travels faster than ever before. Search the title to find it.

Article

"Stop Stealing Black English" — The New York Times (2020)

A powerful opinion piece on how AAVE expressions are adopted and credited — or not — by mainstream white internet culture. Essential reading for the linguistic appropriation discussion.

Website

OED Word of the Year (Annual)

The Oxford English Dictionary's annual Word of the Year announcement is a fascinating snapshot of language change. Past winners: "goblin mode" (2022), "rizz" (2023). Visit oed.com to explore.

Website

Know Your Meme

A comprehensive archive of internet memes and slang with etymological explanations. Useful for tracing where specific terms and expressions come from. Visit knowyourmeme.com.
